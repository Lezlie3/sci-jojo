Part 1

What “Time Stop” Means in JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure

Before we argue with the laws of nature, we need to know what the story is actually claiming. Here’s the idea of Stands and time stop, explained from scratch.

Stands: Battle spirits with superpowers

JoJo lore

In JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure, many characters fight using “Stands”: invisible, spirit‑like beings that only Stand users can see. A Stand is like a personal battle avatar. It can punch, defend, and use special abilities, but it is tied to its user.

If the Stand is hurt, the user is hurt. If the Stand moves, it’s as if the user is acting through it. Different Stands have different powers: some punch very fast, some control fire, some manipulate luck, and a few mess with time itself.

Link: Stand health ↔ user health
Visibility: Only Stand users can see them
Variety: Physical, mental, and time‑based powers
Visual

So what is “time stop” in the story?

The ability

In Part 3, the villain DIO has a Stand called The World, and the hero Jotaro has Star Platinum. Both eventually gain the ability to “stop time.” From their point of view, everything around them freezes: people, bullets, cars, even falling drops of blood.

While time is “stopped,” they can move around, reposition themselves, punch enemies, and move objects. After a few seconds of their personal time, the world resumes, and everyone else experiences all the changes at once.

Step 1
User activates time stop
The world appears to freeze. Only the user and their Stand can move and think.
Step 2
User acts in “frozen” world
They walk, punch, move bullets, reposition objects—everyone else is stuck mid‑motion.
Step 3
Time resumes
To everyone else, time never stopped. They just suddenly feel the punches or see bullets in new places.
In‑universe claim: “Time itself stops for everyone except the user.”

Already suspicious: “Time for everyone but me”

Red flag

Even before we write down equations, there’s a big conceptual problem: time is usually treated as something that flows for the whole universe at once. Saying “time stops for everyone except me” is like saying “gravity pulls on everything except my shoes.”

To see exactly why this is such a problem, we need a tiny bit of physics: what time is, how motion works, and what Newton’s three laws actually say.

Part 2

Time, Motion, and Newton’s Three Laws (From Zero)

Here’s the minimum physics we need: what time means, how we describe motion, and the three rules Newton wrote down that still power most of engineering today.

What “time” means in physics

Concept

In everyday life, time is just “what clocks measure” and “the order of events”—breakfast, then lunch, then dinner. In physics, time is treated as a dimension, like left–right or up–down. Things move not only through space but also from past to future.

Any change—moving, heating up, cooling down, vibrating—happens over time. If time literally stopped, no change at all could happen: no motion, no thinking, no light, nothing.

Key idea
Change needs time.
If there is no time, there is no “before” and “after,” and nothing can move or evolve.

Newton’s three laws in plain language

The rules
1. Inertia If nothing pushes or pulls on an object, it keeps doing what it’s doing. If it’s still, it stays still. If it’s moving, it keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed.
2. Force and acceleration If you push or pull (apply a force), you change how something moves. The rule is:

F = m · a
where F is force, m is mass, and a is acceleration.
3. Action–reaction If you push on something, it pushes back on you equally hard in the opposite direction. You push the ground backward; the ground pushes you forward.
v = distance / time → speed needs time
a = Δv / Δt → acceleration needs time
F = m · a → force depends on acceleration
Part 3

Why a Literal Time Stop Cannot Work

Now we combine the story’s claim (“time stops for everyone except the user”) with the physics we just laid out. We’ll see that basic things like breathing, seeing, and even thinking fall apart.

Air and breathing

Problem #1

Air is made of tiny moving molecules. Breathing works because you expand your chest, and air molecules move in to fill the space. That motion is just particles bouncing around over time.

If time stops, those molecules stop moving. You try to inhale, but nothing can flow into your lungs. You’re effectively trying to move your chest into a solid, frozen block of air.

Breathing as motion
For air molecules:
vair = distance / time
If time → 0, then vair → 0.
No motion → no breathing.

Light and seeing

Problem #2

Light is made of particles called photons that move through space at the speed of light. To see, light has to leave objects, travel to your eyes, and be processed by your brain.

If time stops, photons stop moving. No new light reaches your eyes, and your brain can’t process anything because brain activity also needs time. The result is total darkness and no conscious experience during the “stop.”

Light needs time
Speed of light: c = distance / time
If time is frozen, no distance can be covered. Light cannot travel.

Muscles, nerves, and motion

Problem #3

To move your arm, you need chemical reactions in your muscles and electrical signals in your nerves. All of that is change over time. If time is frozen, no reactions, no signals, no muscle contractions can occur.

That means the user and their Stand would be just as frozen as everyone else. A true global time stop doesn’t let one person move; it locks everything.

Motion as change
Any motion: Δposition ≠ 0
Requires: Δt > 0
If Δt = 0, then Δposition must be 0. No movement.

Newton’s laws collapse

Problem #4

Newton’s laws assume time is flowing. “Keeps moving” or “stays still” only makes sense if time passes. Acceleration is “change in speed per unit time.” No time means no acceleration.

If time is literally stopped, the equations themselves stop making sense. They’re built on the idea that time is a thing that moves forward. A true time stop is not just unlikely—it’s incompatible with the way physics is defined.

A literal “time stop for everyone but me” is not just hard to do—it contradicts the basic definitions of motion and force.
Part 4

Trying to Rescue Time Stop with Math (and Failing)

Some fans try to reinterpret time stop as “the user is just moving incredibly fast.” This uses real ideas from relativity, but it still runs into impossible energy, broken bodies, and shattered causality.

Speed, acceleration, and force

Core equations

Let’s write down the basic math for motion. Don’t worry—we’ll explain each symbol in plain language.

1. Speed (how fast something moves):
v = d / t
where v is speed, d is distance, and t is time.

2. Acceleration (how quickly speed changes):
a = Δv / Δt
where Δv is the change in speed, and Δt is the time it took.

3. Newton’s second law (force):
F = m · a
where F is force, m is mass, and a is acceleration.

If you want someone to go from rest (v = 0) to some huge speed in a tiny amount of time, the acceleration a becomes enormous. That means the required force F becomes enormous too.

Relativity: time slows when you move fast

Einstein enters

Einstein’s special relativity says that when you move very fast (close to the speed of light), time for you slows down compared to someone standing still. To you, your own time feels normal, but the outside world seems sped up.

If you could move at exactly the speed of light (which real objects with mass cannot), time for you would appear to stop. So some people say: maybe the time stop user is just moving incredibly fast, so the world looks frozen.

Relativistic time dilation (conceptual)
Moving fast → your clock ticks slower.
At the speed of light (for massless things like light itself), time doesn’t “pass” in the usual sense.

This is closer to real physics than a literal time stop, but it still causes huge problems: air resistance, shockwaves, insane energy requirements, and a brain that would need to think millions of times faster.

What if we give the user infinite speed?

First what‑if

Let’s push the idea to the extreme: suppose the user can move with infinite speed. That means they can cross any distance in zero time. What does the math say?

Speed definition
v = d / t
Infinite speed → v → ∞.
For that to happen with finite distance d, time t must go to 0.

That means: you cover a non‑zero distance in zero time.

This breaks the idea of speed itself. You’re effectively everywhere along your path at once, or you’re teleporting. It also wrecks causality: in some ways of looking at space and time, you could arrive somewhere before you left.

Force needed for infinite speed
Start at rest: vinitial = 0
End at infinite speed: vfinal → ∞
Acceleration: a = Δv / Δt → ∞ / finite → ∞

Newton’s second law: F = m · a
If m ≠ 0 and a → ∞, then F → ∞.
You need infinite force.

So even our first “what if” (infinite speed) already demands infinite acceleration and infinite force, which no universe with finite energy can provide.

Part 5

Progressively More Insane What‑Ifs

Now we lean into the fun part. We’ll keep granting the time stop user more ridiculous powers and watch which parts of physics break next, from Newton’s laws to causality to the idea of a single universe.

Level 1
What if #1
Infinite speed, but nothing else
We already saw that infinite speed means you cross any distance in zero time. That breaks the definition of speed and lets you arrive somewhere “instantly.” In some viewpoints, you could arrive before you leave, which scrambles cause and effect.

Newton’s second law also explodes: to go from rest to infinite speed in any finite time, your acceleration must be infinite, so the required force is infinite. We’ve broken both the math and the idea that causes must come before effects.
Level 2
What if #2
Infinite speed + infinite force
Suppose we “fix” the previous problem by just giving the user infinite force and infinite acceleration as well. Now they can reach infinite speed without worrying about the required push.

But infinite force means any interaction is catastrophic. Punching someone would accelerate them infinitely fast, effectively tearing their matter out of the normal universe. Touching the ground could shatter the planet. Conservation of energy and momentum are gone, and the user’s energy would curve spacetime so violently that they’d behave like a singularity.
Level 3
What if #3
Ignoring inertia and mass entirely
New rule: the user can change their speed however they like with no force, no inertia, and no reaction on anything else. They can start, stop, and turn instantly without pushing on the ground or the air.

This throws out Newton’s first and third laws. Objects no longer need forces to change motion, and there’s no equal‑and‑opposite reaction. The user becomes a walking exception to the symmetry of the universe, breaking the idea that the laws of physics look the same in every frame of reference.
Level 4
What if #4
Local time bubble
Instead of moving fast, suppose the user controls time in a bubble around themselves. Inside the bubble, time flows normally; outside, time is slowed or stopped. This matches how JoJo often visually presents time stop.

At the bubble’s edge, time flows at different rates on each side, creating a violent “time gradient.” That’s like an extreme version of gravitational time dilation near a black hole, but even sharper. Signals and matter crossing the boundary would behave in bizarre ways, and conservation of energy becomes very hard to maintain when you restart time with objects in new positions and speeds.
Level 5
What if #5
Editing causality itself
Now we go full narrative god mode: the user doesn’t just stop time; they can insert a hidden segment of time where only they act, then force the universe to accept the result as if it had always been that way.

The same past can now lead to different futures depending on whether the user intervenes. The mapping from “past state” to “future state” is no longer unique. We’ve left physics and entered script editing: the user has author‑level privileges over cause and effect.
Level 6
What if #6
Resetting and rewriting the universe
Final boss scenario: the user can stop time, rearrange everything, and then restart in a new universe where their changes are baked into the starting conditions. Each use effectively creates a new branch of reality.

Now we’re not just breaking Newton’s laws or relativity; we’re breaking the idea of a single, continuous universe. Identity, continuity, and even the meaning of “time” become fuzzy. At this point, the user isn’t a physical object at all—they’re more like the writer of the story, editing the timeline from outside.

Direct verdict

Summary

A literal time stop where everything freezes except one person cannot coexist with Newton’s three laws or basic ideas like breathing, seeing, and thinking. Even if we reinterpret it as super speed or relativistic time dilation, we run into impossible energy demands and catastrophic side effects.

The only way to make JoJo‑style time stop “work” is to admit that the user and their Stand are outside the laws of physics, with the power to override or rewrite them. In other words: it’s not a clever use of real‑world mechanics—it’s a narrative superpower, and that’s exactly why it’s so fun.
